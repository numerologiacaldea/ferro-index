In short

The Ferro Index measures whether a hotel is truly luxury, and it does so without relying on subjective reviews: 23 questions about facts observed during the stay and weighed by the standards professional inspectors use, with a 0-100 score split into four bands.

The attention paid to guests is worth 40 points out of 100 on its own. That is not a personal choice: Forbes Travel Guide states that service carries 70 points out of 100 of the rating.

The method

How true luxury is measured

The Ferro Index, unlike the reviews we all know, measures whether the place that hosted you was a luxury hotel or just a stage set.

The difference lies in small but decisive details: they called you by name without you having to remind them, they met a need of yours before you'd asked, they respected your privacy by writing your room number down instead of saying it out loud.

Every fact belongs to one of seven pillars. The weights aren't taste: they follow what the major inspection systems have been observing in the field for years.

The seven pillars are added up the way professional inspectors add them up:

  • what depends on people (attention, privacy, food and cleanliness) is worth 75 points out of 100;
  • what depends on the building (comfort, design and art) is worth 25 points out of 100.

Forbes Travel Guide states that service carries 70 points out of 100: I changed the proportion and kept their order of magnitude.

That choice comes down to a very simple fact: attention is worth 40 points because it is the one item no budget can buy in an afternoon. Marble can be ordered; a service that anticipates a guest's needs takes years to build. You can redo the sum yourself: 40 plus 12 plus 12 plus 11 makes 75 and 12 plus 8 plus 5 makes 25.

Three rules that keep the score honest

Flaws weigh more than merits: every false note you mark takes four points off, because we all know a flat "no" makes you forget ten courtesies.

Thresholds can't be bought: if attention or cleanliness falls under half, the score stays out of the top bands.

Emotion doesn't touch the final score: the last question is only a cross-check.

The proof

Two rooms at the same price, nineteen points apart

An example built to show how the Ferro Index works: these aren't real hotels.

The first has everything a guest loves to photograph: comfort 12 out of 12, design 8 out of 8, art 5 out of 5. But nobody ever called you by name, the dietary preference you gave the waiter never reached the other departments, and the problem you reported was bounced from one colleague to the next. Attention score: 14 out of 40. The overall sum, adding up all the pillars listed above, would be 66, but attention is below 20, less than half its weight, and so the Ferro Index caps the threshold at 64. The Expensive band.

The second has no spa and little on the walls: design 3 out of 8, art 1 out of 5. But it looks after its guests: your coffee arrives exactly the way you take it from day two, your room number was written on the key card sleeve and never said out loud, out of respect for your privacy, and a problem with the shower was fixed straight away, with no passing of the buck. Attention 38 out of 40 which, added to the 7 pillars of the assessment, makes 83. An excellent hotel, two points off Sanctuary.

The premise

Why no reviews

Reviews rest on subjective factors, shaped by each guest's expectations. Someone who rarely travels may reward surprise and leave a glowing comment, while someone who travels constantly can easily tell when it isn't true luxury, but doesn't complain and doesn't write about it: they simply never come back. The scores you read online mix those two factors and hand you a distorted figure.

The Ferro Index, by contrast, rests on what you as a guest can observe: if the facts are there, the luxury is there; if they're not, no glowing review from a stranger can put them there.

The ratings

Stars, Diamonds, Keys: what they actually certify

We often pick a hotel by its stars, but not everyone knows that stars are not a measure of quality: they're an "inventory" of the services a hotel offers, and in fact Italian law, in the decree of 21 October 2008 which sets the national minimum standards, refers to "the services and the fittings" behind the star rating.

Below I've listed the rating systems, so you can see how many concern the fittings and how many the stay itself. Move down the list and you'll notice that the judgement stops being about what the property has and starts being about what happens during the stay.

Who awards hotel stars, and who checks them

In Italy, hotel stars are awarded by the regions; in fact the decree issued by the Presidency of the Council of Ministers on 21 October 2008, published in the Gazzetta Ufficiale on 11 February 2009, only sets national minimum standards. Article 2 makes clear that regions and autonomous provinces are free to raise those standards, and Article 5 also hands them the rulebooks, the checks, the certificate and the ongoing supervision.

There is a second clause that rarely gets any attention, but it matters: Article 3 ties those standards to "the opening of new hotels or the renovation of existing ones", and for hotels already trading it adds that "any obligation to bring structural requirements up to standard is excluded". Which means a historic hotel's star can rest on a building permit from thirty years ago.

What is the difference between a four-star and a five-star hotel

Look at the Italian national schedule and the difference between a four-star and a five-star hotel is smaller than you would guess. For example, a double room goes from a minimum of fifteen square metres for a four-star to sixteen square metres for a five-star, from four to five square metres for the bathroom, from two to three foreign languages spoken at reception or, again, from sixteen to twenty-four hours of opening to the public. Then add parking for eighty per cent of the rooms instead of just fifty, and a service lift.

As you can see for yourself, these are fairly small differences, and they only apply to new builds and renovations; on top of that, regions can always ask for more. And between a one-star and a five-star hotel? One example says it all: the double room grows by two square metres, from fourteen at one star to sixteen at five.

Do hotel stars mean the same thing in every country

They do not, and this is the part that surprises people outside Europe. Over twenty European countries belong to the Hotelstars Union and, in the Union's own words, "classify their hotels on the basis of the same criteria", with procedures aligned to HOTREC principles to guarantee "transparency and comparability of the stars awarded".

Outside that agreement, everyone sets their own. It is the Hotelstars Union itself that explains France and Portugal run national systems of their own, that Spain has seventeen on a regional basis, and that Italy reaches twenty-one, one per region or autonomous province, each carrying the force of law. Italy and France appear on the Union's list as observers. So a four-star in Trentino and a four-star in Sicily answer to two different rulebooks, and neither answers to the one in Vienna.

What are the Michelin Keys

The MICHELIN Guide's distinction for hotels, announced on 5 October 2023 and awarded for the first time in France in April 2024, is based on the following five criteria: architecture and interior design, quality and consistency of service, personality and character, coherence between the experience and the price, and what the hotel adds to its destination.

On 8 October 2025, in Paris, the first worldwide selection arrived: 2,457 hotels, of which 1,742 with one Key, 572 with two and 143 with three.

Two of those five criteria concern consistency of service and character, which is why, among public systems, the Keys come closest to the experience of a stay.

Is a five-star hotel automatically a luxury hotel?

No, and I'll go further: the word "luxury" never appears in the 2008 decree. As you'll have gathered by now, five stars certify the fittings a property provides, not what goes on between the guest and the hotel's staff.

Whoever drafted that decree had noticed it too; in fact Article 6 set up "a rating system, linkable to the stars, allowing the measurement and evaluation of the quality of service provided to customers", voluntary for hotels. Article 8 left its parameters to a later implementing act which, however, does not appear to have ever been adopted.

Ferro Index: what it does and what it doesn't

The Ferro Index does not inspect anonymously, has no inspectors, buys no stays and does not walk into other people's hotels with a notebook. It asks hotel guests to report twenty-three things they experienced first-hand.

Verified in August 2026 against primary sources: the Gazzetta Ufficiale, the MICHELIN Guide, Forbes Travel Guide, LQA and the Hotelstars Union. Regional rules change, and these systems update their own numbers.

The foundations

What the method stands on

  • Forbes Travel Guide: up to 900 standards verified incognito, one by one, yes or no. Forbes states that service carries 70 points out of 100 of the rating, the property 30.
  • LQA, Leading Quality Assurance: over 1,000 standards per stay, alongside a reading of how the service felt. The report goes to the hotel, not the guest.
  • Michelin Keys: five stated criteria, two of which concern consistency of service and character and not fittings.
  • AAA Five Diamond: requirements that rule you out before the score is even considered, checked by unannounced annual inspections.
  • Academic research: from SERVQUAL onwards, the studies keep repeating the same thing: how people behave predicts perceived quality better than any other material.

The Ferro Index translates those systems into questions for guests, and filters them through one precise idea, that luxury comes down to three things: time, exclusivity and care. Everything else is price.

Questions

FAQ: what we often get asked

How can I tell if a hotel is truly luxury?

Look at facts, not declared stars: do they call you by name unprompted? Does something arrive before you ask? Is your room number written down and never said out loud? Is the juice squeezed to order? The Ferro Index is made up of twenty-three questions about facts like these and turns the answers into a score.

Why aren't reviews enough?

Because they average people who are amazed by everything with people who never complain. True luxury isn't judged by other people's average: it's verified with objective facts.

What matters most?

Attention is worth 40% of the score, and the reason is very simple: fine materials can be bought in an afternoon, a service that anticipates guests' needs takes years to build.

What is the Ferro Index, in one sentence?
